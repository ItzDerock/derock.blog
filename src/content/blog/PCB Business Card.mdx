## so.. what does it do?

It's a spectrum analyzer. The array of 8 LEDs divide up the 2.4GHz spectrum
band, and glow according to how much power is on each band. Start playing some
music over Bluetooth, and you'll see more activity over the Bluetooth portion of
the spectrum. You can get a sense of how much noise some WiFi channels have to
help pick the right 2.4GHz band. The microprocessor on the board also supports
Bluetooth, so plug it into your laptop and start using it to sniff or send BLE
packets. With GPIO and ST-Link broken out, you can reflash this board and use it
as a battery-operated microcontroller for an IoT project.

There is a NFC coil on the front, so you can tap the card to see who made it
(me!). A ruler on the back for quick measurements, alongside common SMD
footprints so you can gauge how much space an IC will take. And, of course, a
cat to remind you about the species that actually rules the world.

The board contains two small trace antenna and a Nordic nRF52840. One antenna is
tuned for NFC at 13.56MHz, with the other antenna tuned for 2.4GHz. The nRF52840
supports a range of 2360MHz to 2500MHz across multiple protocols: Bluetooth Low
Energy (BLE), Bluetooth mesh, Thread, Zigbee, IEEE 802.15.4, ANT/ANT+, and
Nordic's proprietary 2.4GHz wireless protocols.

The IEEE 802.15.4 support is what allows for this board to run as a crude
spectrum analyzer, as the standard requires the radio to perform RSSI detection
on a channel to find the one with least interference. For the card, I used that
energy detect function to measure the energy within 5MHz channel buckets.

## how (hardware)

I started with deciding what microcontroller to use, and needed something super
low power. ESP32s and RP2040/2042 W are quite efficient chips with wireless, but
still not efficient enough, since the CR2032 coincell battery only has a
capacity of around 230mAh. When you need efficiency, you go nordic. They make
some seriously efficient chips, with the nrf52840 only needing like 5-10mAh
during radio transmissions compared to the esp32 family which need around 10x
that. Same goes for sleep, an ESP32 typically draws 5-10uA compared to nrf52840
with only 0.5uA of deep sleep current draw. The nrf52840 also supports NFC so I
can save some components on the BOM.

I had actually initially spec'd the nrf52832, but found that availability for
the nrf52840 was much better with my PCBA supplier (JLC) while being only
margionally cheaper, so I swapped to the bigger nRF52840.
